3 Hint: Tutorial on Breaking the Vigenere Cipher `
This tutorial is to help you understand the classical attack against the Vigenere cipher. The `
classical attack against the Vigenere cipher is known as ` Kasiski’s Method. The attack consists
of two independent stages.
1. Estimate the length of the secret key.
2. Recover each letter of the secret key individually.
Once the secret key has been recovered, decrypting the ciphertext becomes straightforward.
Suppose the secret key is MATH, whose length is l = 4. During encryption, the key is repeated.
For example,

Key M A T H M A T H M A T H

Breaking one Vigenere cipher is equivalent to breaking ` l independent shift ciphers.

3.1.1 Estimating the Key Length Using IoC
Assume the candidate key length is l. Split the ciphertext into l slices. For example, if l = 4,

S1 : C0, C4, C8, C12, . . .
S2 : C1, C5, C9, C13, . . .
S3 : C2, C6, C10, C14, . . .
S4 : C3, C7, C11, C15, . . .

If the guessed key length is correct, each slice has been encrypted using a single shift
cipher and therefore resembles ordinary English. Consequently, its IoC will be close to that of
English. If the guessed key length is incorrect, each slice mixes several shift ciphers together,
producing text that is closer to random. Consequently, its IoC will be much smaller. Therefore,
we compute the average IoC for every candidate key length and select the smallest candidate
whose average IoC is close to that of English.

3.1.2 Algorithm for Estimating the Key Length
Input: Ciphertext C, maximum key length L.
Output: Estimated key length l.
1. For every candidate key length j = 1, 2, . . . , L:
(a) Split the ciphertext into j slices.
(b) Compute the IoC of every slice.
(c) Compute the average IoC.
2. Return the smallest key length whose average IoC is closest to English.
